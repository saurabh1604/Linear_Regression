Linear Regression

A Visual Introduction to the Workhorse of Machine Learning

1. The Problem

Imagine we want to predict the price of a house based on its size. We have some historical data: points on a 2D plane where the x-axis is Square Footage and the y-axis is Price.

Our goal is to find a model—a mathematical equation—that describes the relationship between these two variables. In Linear Regression, we assume this relationship is a straight line:

$$ y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \epsilon $$

Where \( \beta_0 \) is the intercept (bias), \( \beta_1 \) is the slope (weight), and \( \epsilon \) is the noise/error.

2. The Baseline Model

Before we try to find a fancy line, let's start with the simplest possible prediction: the Average. If we ignore the size of the house, our best guess for any house is just the average price of all houses in our dataset.

The horizontal line represents \( \bar{y} \) (the mean). The vertical lines are the residuals (errors). Drag the points to see how the mean and residuals change.

3. Fitting the Line & Minimizing Squares

The "Mean" model is okay, but we can do better by using the house size. We want a line that passes "through" the data. But what does "best fit" actually mean?

It means we want to minimize the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE). Visually, we want to make the total area of the squares formed by the residuals as small as possible.

Try it! Drag the two circular handles on the line to adjust the Slope (\( \beta_1 \)) and Intercept (\( \beta_0 \)). Try to make the red squares as small as possible and get R² close to 1.

MSE (Mean Squared Error) measures the average squared difference between the estimated values and the actual value. R² (Coefficient of Determination) represents the proportion of the variance for a dependent variable that's explained by an independent variable. Higher R² (up to 1.0) is better.

4. How the Machine Learns (Gradient Descent)

You just fit the line manually. But how does a computer do it? It uses an algorithm called Gradient Descent. Imagine a landscape where the "height" is the Error (MSE). We want to walk downhill to find the lowest point.

Left: The regression line updating. Right: The "Cost Landscape" (Contour Plot). The dot represents our current model parameters (\( \beta_0, \beta_1 \)). Watch it roll down the hill!

What if we have two features, \( X_1 \) and \( X_2 \), but they are highly correlated (e.g., House Size in sqft and House Size in sq meters)? The model gets confused about which feature gets the credit.
